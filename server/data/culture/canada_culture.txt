Culture Guide: Canada

Social Norms:
Canadians are known internationally for being polite, friendly, and apologetic. Saying "sorry" is extremely common — even when something is not your fault. This is not sarcasm or weakness; it is genuine cultural politeness. Holding doors open for people behind you is standard — failing to do so is considered rude.

Canada is officially bilingual (English and French). Outside Quebec, English dominates. In Quebec, French is the official language and primary language of daily life. In Quebec, always attempt to speak French first, even if your French is limited — switching to English is more accepted if you make the effort first.

Canadian society is highly multicultural and generally welcoming to international students. Major cities like Toronto, Vancouver, Montreal, Calgary, and Ottawa have large international communities.

Indirect communication is valued. Canadians tend to avoid confrontation and will often soften criticism or disagreement. Direct bluntness (common in some European cultures) can come across as rude. Patience and understatement are appreciated.

Tipping:
Tipping norms in Canada are similar to the United States but slightly lower.
- Restaurants: 15 to 20% is standard. Some card machines suggest 18%, 20%, and 25%.
- Bars: 1 to 2 CAD per drink or 15 to 18% on a tab
- Taxi and rideshare: 10 to 15%
- Food delivery: 10 to 15%
- Hotel housekeeping: 2 to 5 CAD per night
- Coffee shops: optional — 1 CAD if you feel it is warranted

Tipping is not expected at fast food restaurants or self-checkout.

Transport:
Public transport quality varies significantly by city. Toronto (TTC), Montreal (STM), and Vancouver (TransLink) have reasonably good metro and bus systems. Calgary and Edmonton have light rail (LRT). Smaller cities rely primarily on buses.

Most universities are connected to city transit. Student transit passes (U-Pass or equivalent) are often included in student fees or available at a discount — check with your university. Winters are severe in most of Canada — buses and the metro are essential when temperatures drop to -20°C or below.

Cycling is popular in warmer months. Most major cities have dedicated bike lanes. In winter, cycling becomes impractical or dangerous in most Canadian cities.

Weather:
Canadian winters are serious — temperatures regularly drop to -10°C to -30°C in most of the country from December to March. Pack a proper winter coat, insulated boots, thermal layers, gloves, and a hat before your first winter. Vancouver and Victoria on the west coast have milder, rainy winters. Do not underestimate the cold — frostbite is a genuine risk in extreme temperatures.

Food:
Major supermarket chains: Loblaws, Sobeys, Metro, Safeway (west), IGA (Quebec). Budget options: No Frills, Food Basics, FreshCo, Walmart Supercentre. Canadian grocery prices are generally higher than the US. Farmers markets are available in most cities in spring and summer.

Poutine (fries, cheese curds, and gravy) is a Canadian staple. Tim Hortons is the national coffee chain — very affordable and ubiquitous. BeaverTails (fried pastry) and butter tarts are iconic Canadian foods.

SIM Cards:
Canadian mobile data is among the most expensive in the world. Major carriers: Bell, Rogers, Telus. Budget options (MVNOs): Koodo (Telus), Fido (Rogers), Lucky Mobile (Bell), Public Mobile (Telus). Public Mobile often has the best prepaid rates — around 34 CAD per month for a reasonable data plan. Buy SIMs at carrier stores, Walmart, or Best Buy. You will need a Canadian address to register.

Banking:
Open a Canadian bank account promptly. Major banks (the "Big Five"): RBC, TD, Scotiabank, BMO, CIBC. Most offer no-fee student accounts with proof of enrolment. You will need your passport, study permit, and a Canadian address. Interac e-Transfer is the standard way to send money between Canadian bank accounts.

Provincial Health Insurance:
Some provinces cover international students under provincial health insurance after a waiting period (British Columbia after 3 months, Manitoba immediately). Others do not cover international students at all (Ontario, Alberta, Quebec). Check your province's rules and ensure you have private health insurance if you are not covered. Your university's student health plan typically covers this gap.
