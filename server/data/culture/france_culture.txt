Culture Guide: France

Social Norms:
Greetings are important in France. When entering a shop, pharmacy, or small business, always say "Bonjour" (good morning/day) or "Bonsoir" (good evening) to the staff. Leaving without saying "Au revoir" (goodbye) is considered rude. Failing to greet people properly is one of the most common cultural mistakes made by foreigners in France.

Among people who know each other, the cheek kiss greeting (la bise) is standard. The number of kisses varies by region — typically two (one on each cheek), but sometimes one or four. Wait for the French person to initiate and follow their lead.

French people tend to be formal with strangers and acquaintances and use "vous" (formal you) rather than "tu" (informal you) until explicitly invited to use "tu." Use "vous" with teachers, administrators, shopkeepers, and anyone you have just met.

Meals are cultural rituals. The French take lunch seriously — a proper sit-down lunch of at least an hour is normal. Dinner is typically eaten late, around 7:30 to 8:30pm. Eating on the go or while walking is considered uncouth by older generations, though younger French people are more relaxed about this.

Complaining in restaurants is handled very differently from the UK. If food is wrong, calmly and politely tell the waiter — do not make a scene. French service culture can seem cold or slow by Anglo-American standards; it is not intentionally rude.

Tipping:
Service is legally included in restaurant bills in France (service compris). Tipping is not obligatory. If you are happy with the service, leaving a few euros (2 to 5 EUR) is appreciated but not expected. Rounding up for café coffees and taxis is common.

Transport:
France has excellent public transport, especially in Paris. The Paris Métro covers the city comprehensively. The RER (suburban rail) connects Paris to the surrounding region and to both main airports (CDG and Orly). Buy a Navigo Découverte travel card for unlimited travel on Métro, RER, buses, and trams within zones.

Students under 26 are eligible for significant discounts on SNCF (national rail) with the Carte Avantage Jeune (approximately 49 EUR per year) — saves 30 to 60% on most train fares.

In most French cities outside Paris, buses and trams are the main public transport. Cycling is common in cities like Bordeaux, Lyon, Strasbourg, and Nantes.

Food:
France has a strong food culture. Markets (marchés) are held regularly in most towns and neighbourhoods and offer fresh, affordable produce. Boulangeries (bakeries) are everywhere — a fresh baguette costs less than 1 EUR. University restaurants (Restaurants Universitaires, RU) are subsidised by CROUS and offer hot meals for approximately 3.30 EUR for enrolled students — significantly cheaper than any restaurant.

Large supermarkets: Carrefour, Leclerc, Intermarché, Casino, Monoprix. Lidl and Aldi are available in larger cities. Most supermarkets are closed on Sundays (or close early). Plan grocery shopping for Saturday.

SIM Cards:
Prepaid SIM cards are available at supermarkets, tabacs (newsagents/tobacco shops), and phone shops. Popular prepaid providers: Free Mobile (excellent value), SFR, Orange, Bouygues. You need to show ID (passport) to buy a SIM in France. Register online with your French address once you have one.

Language:
While many French people in cities, especially younger generations, speak English, France has a strong cultural attachment to the French language. Making an effort to speak French — even imperfectly — is greatly appreciated. Starting any interaction with "Bonjour, parlez-vous anglais?" (Hello, do you speak English?) before switching to English is the correct approach. Attempting to speak French will be met with patience and often encouragement.

Banking:
Opening a French bank account as an international student requires your passport, student card, proof of address in France, and a French phone number. Traditional banks: BNP Paribas, Crédit Agricole, Société Générale, La Banque Postale (easiest for new arrivals — apply at any post office). Online banks with easier signup: N26, Revolut (not a full bank but useful). Many French landlords require a French bank account for rent payments — open one as soon as possible after arrival.
