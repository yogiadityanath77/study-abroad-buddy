Culture Guide: Germany

Social Norms:
Germans value punctuality very highly. Arriving late to a meeting, class, or social event — even by a few minutes — is considered disrespectful. If you are running late, call or message ahead.

Personal space is important. Germans generally maintain a formal distance with people they do not know well. Greetings with acquaintances are typically a firm handshake. Close friends may hug or kiss on the cheek, but this is not standard with new people.

German communication style is direct. People say what they mean without excessive softening. This can feel blunt or even rude to people from cultures where indirect communication is the norm, but it is not intended as rudeness — it is considered honest and respectful.

Noise and quiet hours (Ruhezeit) are taken seriously. Quiet hours are typically from 10pm to 7am and on Sundays. Avoid loud music, parties, or noisy activities during these times or you risk complaints from neighbours and potentially fines.

Recycling and waste separation is a civic duty. Every household separates waste into categories: paper (blue bin), packaging/plastics (yellow bin), organic waste (brown bin), residual waste (grey/black bin), and glass (separate containers by colour). Learn your building's system when you arrive.

Tipping:
Tipping in restaurants is expected but not obligatory. The standard is to round up the bill or add 5 to 10%. Tell the server how much you want to pay when they bring the card machine or cash — do not leave money on the table after they have walked away. In bars, rounding up is common. Tip taxi drivers by rounding up.

Transport:
Germany has excellent public transport. In most cities, the U-Bahn (underground), S-Bahn (suburban rail), tram, and bus are integrated under one ticketing system. Buy a monthly student pass (Semesterticket) — most universities include this in semester fees. Always validate your ticket before boarding — ticket inspectors (Kontrolleure) work undercover and fines for travelling without a valid ticket are approximately 60 EUR.

Cycling is extremely common in Germany, especially in cities like Berlin, Munich, Hamburg, and Freiburg. Many students cycle to university. Use dedicated cycle lanes and follow traffic rules — cycling on the pavement (sidewalk) is often illegal.

Food:
German supermarkets include REWE, EDEKA, Aldi, Lidl, Penny, and Netto. Aldi and Lidl are the most affordable options. Most supermarkets are closed on Sundays — plan ahead. Bakeries (Bäckerei) are everywhere and excellent for cheap, filling breakfast. University canteens (Mensa) offer subsidised hot meals for enrolled students for 3 to 6 EUR — use your student card.

SIM Cards and Mobile Phone:
Buy a prepaid SIM card (Prepaid-SIM) at supermarkets (Aldi Talk, Lidl Connect, REWE Mobil), electronics stores (Saturn, MediaMarkt), or mobile phone shops. You will need your passport for registration. Providers on major networks: Telekom, Vodafone, O2. To register a German SIM you must provide a German address — use your student dormitory or accommodation address.

Language:
Most Germans under 40 in cities speak reasonable to excellent English. In smaller towns and with older generations, English proficiency is lower. Learning basic German phrases is appreciated and will help you integrate. Most university courses for international students are available in English, especially at Master's level.

Banking:
Open a German bank account early. Popular options for students and internationals: N26 (fully online, English interface), DKB (free account, good for travel), Commerzbank or Sparkasse (traditional banks with local branches). You will need a registered German address. Most accounts can be opened with your passport and registration certificate (Anmeldung).
