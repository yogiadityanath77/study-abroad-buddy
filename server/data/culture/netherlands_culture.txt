Culture Guide: Netherlands

Social Norms:
The Dutch are known for being direct, honest, and pragmatic. Dutch communication is very straightforward — people say exactly what they mean without excessive softening or diplomatic padding. A Dutch person saying "your presentation was not very clear" is not being rude; they are being honest. This directness can feel blunt or even harsh to people from more indirect cultures. Over time you will come to appreciate it as genuine and time-saving.

Egalitarianism is deeply embedded in Dutch culture. Hierarchy is flat — Dutch students call their professors by first name, and authority is questioned and debated openly. Academic and professional environments are collegial rather than formal.

The Dutch have a concept called "doe maar gewoon, dan doe je al gek genoeg" — roughly translated as "just act normally, that is already crazy enough." Showing off, being ostentatious, or drawing unnecessary attention to yourself is frowned upon. Humility and moderation are valued.

Punctuality is important. Arriving on time for meetings, classes, and social events is expected. Cancelling plans at short notice — especially for dinner invitations — is considered very rude.

Directness extends to invitations: if a Dutch person invites you to something, they mean it. If they say they are busy, they are busy — they will not make vague plans just to be polite.

Tipping:
Tipping in the Netherlands is appreciated but not obligatory. Service is included in the price under Dutch law.
- Restaurants: rounding up or leaving 5 to 10% for good service is appreciated
- Cafés and coffee shops (not the cannabis kind — those are called coffeeshops): not expected
- Taxis: round up to the nearest euro
- Bars: not expected
- Hairdressers: 1 to 2 EUR if you are happy

Tipping is never obligatory and no one will be offended if you do not tip.

Transport:
The Netherlands has world-class public transport and cycling infrastructure. The OV-chipkaart is the smart card used for all public transport — trains (NS), trams, buses, and metro. Buy one at train stations and supermarkets. Always check in and check out when boarding and leaving — failing to check out results in being charged the maximum fare.

Cycling (fietsen) is the primary mode of transport in most Dutch cities. Amsterdam, Utrecht, Delft, Leiden, Groningen, and Eindhoven all have extensive cycle networks. Most students cycle everywhere. Buy a second-hand bicycle (fiets) as soon as possible — new bikes are frequently stolen. A basic second-hand bike costs 50 to 150 EUR. Always use lights at night (legally required) and lock your bike with at least two locks.

Trains connect all major cities reliably. The NS (Nederlandse Spoorwegen) app is essential. Students can get an OV-jaarkaart (public transport subscription) — ask your university about student travel discounts.

Weather:
The Netherlands has a maritime climate — mild, wet, and windy year-round. Rain is frequent and can come suddenly at any time. Always carry a small foldable umbrella or a waterproof jacket. Winters are grey and damp rather than very cold (typically 0 to 7°C). Summers are warm but not hot (18 to 25°C). Wind is almost constant — umbrellas frequently break; a good waterproof jacket with a hood is more practical.

Food:
Traditional Dutch food is hearty and simple — stamppot (mashed potato with vegetables), erwtensoep (split pea soup), bitterballen (deep-fried beef ragout balls), stroopwafels, and Dutch cheese (Gouda, Edam). Dutch portions are practical rather than generous.

Supermarkets: Albert Heijn (premium, everywhere), Jumbo (good quality, competitive prices), Lidl and Aldi (cheapest). Most supermarkets are open 7 days a week. City centres have AH To Go convenience stores open late.

SIM Cards:
Buy a prepaid SIM at a phone shop, electronics store (MediaMarkt), or supermarket. Major carriers: KPN, Vodafone, T-Mobile Netherlands. Budget prepaid options: Simyo (KPN network), Ben (T-Mobile). A decent prepaid plan costs 10 to 20 EUR per month. You will need to register with a Dutch address. eSIMs are widely available.

Banking:
Dutch banks are generally not easy for international students to open accounts with immediately. ING and ABN AMRO offer student accounts. Bunq is a Dutch online bank that is easier to open as an international student. Revolut and N26 are popular alternatives used by many international students while waiting for a Dutch account. You will need a BSN number for a full Dutch bank account — you get your BSN after registering with the municipality.

Dutch Directness — A Final Note:
When a Dutch person asks "how are you?" they may actually want to know. When they tell you something is wrong, take it seriously and do not be offended. When they are warm and friendly to you, it is genuine — the Dutch do not waste warmth on people they do not like.
